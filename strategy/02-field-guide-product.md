## Front Matter

**Welcome.**

Every concept in this guide is real — a named, established psychological mechanism, not a vibe or a horoscope. You don't need a psych degree to use it. Each entry follows the same structure:

- **What it is** — plain English, no jargon.
- **Where you've seen it** — a real, relatable scenario.
- **Try this** — one thing you can actually do with it today.

This isn't a diagnostic tool and it isn't therapy. If something in here touches on a pattern that feels heavier than "interesting to know," a licensed therapist is the right next step — this guide is a starting point for understanding, not a substitute for care.

Read it in one sitting, or keep it as a reference and come back to one concept at a time. Either way — welcome to the code.

## Chapter 1 — Understanding Yourself

### 1. Loss Aversion
**What it is:** Losing something hurts roughly twice as much, psychologically, as gaining the equivalent amount feels good. Your brain isn't rational about gains and losses — it's protective.
**Where you've seen it:** Losing $20 ruins your afternoon. Finding $20 barely registers by dinner. Same amount, opposite weight.
**Try this:** Before a "limited time" decision, ask: "Would I want this at this price if it weren't framed as a loss?" Strip the urgency and re-decide.

### 2. Sunk Cost Fallacy
**What it is:** We keep investing in something *because* we've already invested in it — even when continuing is the worse choice. Past cost isn't a reason to add future cost.
**Where you've seen it:** Finishing a bad movie because you're "already an hour in." Staying in a job or relationship because of "everything I've put into this."
**Try this:** Ask, "If I were starting today, knowing what I know now, would I choose this?" If the answer is no, the sunk cost is gone either way — the only live question is what happens next.

### 3. Cognitive Dissonance
**What it is:** Holding two conflicting beliefs about yourself (e.g., "I'm smart" and "I made a bad call") is so uncomfortable that your brain edits the story — usually by defending the decision instead of admitting the mistake.
**Where you've seen it:** Over-explaining an expensive purchase you regret. Defending a decision more passionately than the situation calls for.
**Try this:** Notice the urge to over-justify. Ask what you're actually protecting — usually it's self-image, not the decision itself. Naming it reduces its grip.

### 4. Spotlight Effect
**What it is:** We wildly overestimate how much other people notice and remember about us, because we're the main character in our own head — not in theirs.
**Where you've seen it:** Replaying an embarrassing comment from years ago that the other person has completely forgotten.
**Try this:** Next time you're spiraling over how you "looked," remember: the math is genuinely on your side. Everyone else is starring in their own spotlight effect, not yours.

### 5. Negativity Bias
**What it is:** Evolutionarily, threats mattered more for survival than praise, so your brain over-weights negative input compared to positive input of equal size.
**Where you've seen it:** 40 kind comments, 1 harsh one — you remember the 1.
**Try this:** When it happens, name it out loud: "this is negativity bias, not truth." Studies show simply labeling the bias measurably reduces its pull.

### 6. Dunning-Kruger Effect
**What it is:** Low competence often comes bundled with a low ability to *recognize* that low competence — so confidence peaks early in learning something, then dips as real skill (and awareness of the gap) grows.
**Where you've seen it:** Beginner confidence at chess, cooking, or public speaking, right before it should exist.
**Try this:** Rising self-doubt after you start learning something seriously isn't a bad sign — it's usually proof you're getting a more accurate read on the size of the topic.

### 7. Hedonic Adaptation
**What it is:** Humans re-baseline quickly to new circumstances, good or bad — a raise, a new car, or a breakup all feel less intense after the adjustment period than they did on day one.
**Where you've seen it:** The excitement of a purchase or promotion fading within weeks, even though nothing about it changed.
**Try this:** Spend on experiences, time-saving, and giving rather than more stuff — research consistently shows these resist hedonic adaptation better than material purchases.

### 8. Temporal Discounting
**What it is:** We value immediate rewards far more than future ones, even when the future reward is objectively bigger — future-you can feel like a stranger you're not obligated to help.
**Where you've seen it:** Procrastinating a task that would take 20 minutes today into a stressful 2am scramble tomorrow.
**Try this:** Make the future cost concrete and near-term ("if I don't start now, tonight will be worse"), rather than abstract and far away.

### 9. Fresh Start Effect
**What it is:** We're more motivated to pursue goals right after a "temporal landmark" — a new week, month, year, or birthday — because it psychologically separates us from past failures.
**Where you've seen it:** New Year's resolutions, "starting Monday," a fresh notebook for a new habit.
**Try this:** Don't wait for January. Manufacture your own fresh start (new week, new month) whenever you need the motivational reset — the effect works regardless of the calendar's actual significance.

## Chapter 2 — Relationships & Dating

### 10. Fawning
**What it is:** A trauma-response pattern where keeping the peace — including over-apologizing — becomes a learned way to stay safe, not genuine politeness.
**Where you've seen it:** Saying "sorry" when a friend cancels on *you*.
**Try this:** Swap the reflexive apology for a neutral response: "No worries — let's reschedule." No apology required for someone else's choice.

### 11. Mere-Exposure Effect
**What it is:** Repeated exposure alone makes your brain relabel something as more likeable, regardless of its actual qualities.
**Where you've seen it:** A coworker who was mildly annoying in week one becoming a real friend by week six — nothing about them changed.
**Try this:** If you want to build a relationship or habit, prioritize small, repeated contact over one big gesture — proximity beats intensity.

### 12. Attachment Styles
**What it is:** Four broad patterns (secure, anxious, avoidant, disorganized) rooted in early bonding experiences that echo into adult relationships — texting habits, conflict responses, comfort with closeness.
**Where you've seen it:** One partner needs constant reassurance (anxious), the other needs space to process (avoidant) — same conflict, opposite instincts.
**Try this:** This is a tendency, not a life sentence — "earned security" is a real, researched outcome of consistent healthy relationships and self-work, not a fixed trait.

### 13. Benjamin Franklin Effect
**What it is:** Doing something kind *for* someone increases their fondness for you — but asking *them* for a small favor can work even better, because their brain looks for a reason why they helped and lands on "I must like them."
**Where you've seen it:** Feeling closer to someone right after they helped you with something small.
**Try this:** Small, genuine asks ("can I get your opinion on this?") build more real rapport than most people expect from such a minor interaction.

### 14. Love-Bombing
**What it is:** An intense, accelerated wave of affection and attention early in a relationship — flattering in the moment, but sometimes used (consciously or not) to fast-track trust before it's earned.
**Where you've seen it:** A new relationship moving from "hello" to "soulmate" language within days.
**Try this:** Intensity isn't proof of depth. Let consistency over time, not velocity at the start, be the signal you weight most.

### 15. Gaslighting
**What it is:** A pattern where someone repeatedly denies or distorts your clear memory, perception, or feelings, causing you to doubt your own judgment.
**Where you've seen it:** Being told "that never happened" about something you clearly remember, repeatedly, until you start to doubt yourself.
**Try this:** The gut-check: "Am I being asked to doubt something I clearly saw, heard, or felt?" If yes repeatedly, that's information worth taking seriously — and worth talking through with someone outside the situation.

### 16. Parasocial Relationships
**What it is:** A one-sided bond that feels mutual because repeated, intimate-feeling exposure (like watching a creator regularly) is processed by your brain similarly to a real friendship.
**Where you've seen it:** Feeling like you "know" a YouTuber or podcaster you've never met.
**Try this:** Not inherently unhealthy — the useful question is whether it's supplementing your real, reciprocal connections or quietly replacing them.

## Chapter 3 — Work, Money & Negotiation

### 17. Anchoring Bias
**What it is:** The first number mentioned in a negotiation sets the mental range for everything that follows, even when it shouldn't.
**Where you've seen it:** Naming your salary range first in an interview — even a modest number becomes the ceiling in the other side's head.
**Try this:** Where possible, let the other side anchor first ("what's the budget for this role?") and negotiate up from their number instead of down from yours.

### 18. Reciprocity
**What it is:** Humans are wired to repay what they receive, even small, unsolicited things — one of the oldest and most reliable levers in influence.
**Where you've seen it:** A free sample making a purchase feel less like a sale and more like a fair exchange.
**Try this (ethical use):** Give real value first with no strings attached, and let goodwill build naturally. This is the same mechanism marketers use — the difference is whether the gift is genuine or a manufactured trap.

### 19. IKEA Effect
**What it is:** Effort creates ownership, and ownership inflates perceived value — regardless of actual quality.
**Where you've seen it:** Defending your wobbly self-assembled bookshelf like it's furniture royalty.
**Try this:** With teams or kids, let people build a small piece of the work themselves. Buy-in isn't persuaded — it's built.

### 20. Pratfall Effect
**What it is:** A small, visible mistake makes an *already-competent* person seem more likeable and human — but it backfires on someone who hasn't demonstrated competence yet.
**Where you've seen it:** The polished coworker who trips and laughs it off becomes more liked, not less.
**Try this:** Don't hide every flaw once you're established. A well-handled stumble builds trust — but earn the competence first.

### 21. Primacy Effect
**What it is:** Information (and impressions) presented first is remembered and weighted more heavily than what comes later.
**Where you've seen it:** A strong opening line in an interview or date shaping the entire rest of the interaction's tone.
**Try this:** Front-load your strongest, clearest point in any pitch, interview, or first meeting — don't save the best for last.

### 22. Warmth vs. Competence
**What it is:** People judge you almost instantly on two independent axes — how warm/trustworthy you seem, and how capable/competent you seem — and warmth is usually processed first and weighted more in everyday likability.
**Where you've seen it:** "Confidence" advice that actually reads as coldness backfiring in interviews and first dates.
**Try this:** Lead with warmth (genuine interest in the other person) before leading with credentials — it changes how your competence gets received afterward.

### 23. Halo Effect
**What it is:** One positive trait (like attractiveness or confidence) unconsciously colors judgment of unrelated traits (like honesty or skill).
**Where you've seen it:** The same resume, same mistake — the more conventionally attractive candidate rated as "more competent" by interviewers unaware of the bias.
**Try this:** When you notice fast admiration for someone, ask: "impressed by what, specifically?" Separating the traits catches the halo before it colors the whole judgment.

## Chapter 4 — Groups, Society & Everyday Life

### 24. Social Proof
**What it is:** When uncertain, we outsource decisions to what everyone else appears to be doing, assuming the crowd knows something we don't.
**Where you've seen it:** Walking into the crowded restaurant over the empty one next door without checking either menu.
**Try this:** Use it as a check, not a crutch — ask *why* the crowd is there before joining it. Sometimes it's quality. Sometimes it's just a crowd.

### 25. Fundamental Attribution Error
**What it is:** We explain our own behavior by circumstance ("I was late, that's not usually like me") and everyone else's by character ("what a jerk") — same action, opposite explanation, depending on who did it.
**Where you've seen it:** Getting cut off in traffic and immediately judging the driver's character, not their circumstances.
**Try this:** Ask what circumstance might explain someone else's frustrating behavior — the same grace you'd automatically give yourself.

### 26. Bystander Effect
**What it is:** Responsibility diffuses across a crowd — the more witnesses present, the *less* likely any single person is to help, because everyone assumes someone else will act.
**Where you've seen it:** Large crowds being statistically slower to respond to someone needing help than a single bystander would be.
**Try this:** If you ever need help in public, don't shout "somebody help" — point at one specific person and say "you, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance." Naming someone collapses the diffusion instantly.

### 27. Confirmation Bias
**What it is:** We don't process new information neutrally — we hunt for evidence that supports what we already believe and scrutinize (or dismiss) anything that doesn't.
**Where you've seen it:** Sending someone a solid source and watching them find a reason to dismiss it rather than update their view.
**Try this:** You can't out-fact a feeling. Curiosity-first questions ("what would change your mind?") land where direct contradiction usually bounces off.

### 28. Groupthink
**What it is:** Group settings create pressure toward consensus over accuracy — dissenting views get suppressed, sometimes without anyone consciously deciding to suppress them.
**Where you've seen it:** A team agreeing to a plan nobody individually loved, because no one wanted to be the lone objection.
**Try this:** Explicitly invite disagreement before a group decision locks in — ask "what's the strongest argument against this?" out loud, every time.

### 29. Online Disinhibition Effect
**What it is:** Physical and social distance online lowers normal social filters — the same person often behaves very differently in a group chat or comments section than face-to-face.
**Where you've seen it:** A reasonable person's tone shifting sharply once a disagreement moves into text.
**Try this:** Before sending a heated message, ask: "would I say this exact sentence to their face, in this exact tone?" If not, slow down before sending.

### 30. Zeigarnik Effect
**What it is:** Your brain treats incomplete tasks as "open loops" it won't fully release until there's closure — real or simulated.
**Where you've seen it:** A half-finished work task still occupying your mind at 11pm.
**Try this:** Before stopping work on something, write down the very next physical step. Your brain treats that written placeholder as enough closure to release the tension.
